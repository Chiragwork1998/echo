# Breathe: sources, evidence and safety

The record behind every practice in `src/features/breathe/library/practices.ts`: where each idea
comes from, what its source actually teaches, how strong the evidence is, what ECHO must never
claim, and what to watch for safety. Tests check the practices' shape, timing and copy; they cannot
check this, so read it again whenever a practice, a line or a "why" changes.

## Rules every practice keeps

- **Paraphrase only.** Nothing in the app quotes a book, a talk or a paper. A name appears only to
  credit where an idea comes from. No author has reviewed or endorsed ECHO, and no copy may suggest
  one has.
- **No claim to treat anything.** A practice is "for when you feel anxious", never "for anxiety".
- **A "why" is one or two true sentences.** Where it cites a study, the study is below with its size
  and limits, and the sentence says no more than the study found.

Evidence is graded in plain words:

- **Good:** a meta-analysis or several controlled trials pointing the same way.
- **Some:** one or a few small controlled studies, or lab studies of the same thing.
- **Thin:** uncontrolled or very low quality studies, or lab findings about a nearby mechanism.
- **Teaching only:** a respected teacher's method or book, with no direct trials.

Citations were checked on 2026-11-08 by web search against journal, PubMed, publisher and library
records: authors, title, journal, year. Books were checked by their publisher and catalogue records,
tables of contents and secondary summaries, not read in full. ECHO's wording is its own throughout.

## Corrections to the brief

1. **Ready** is credited in the brief to Maxwell Maltz alone, but its first two minutes are box
   breathing, which One thing credits to Mark Divine. The library credits both.
2. **Arrive** is credited in the brief to Maltz and Joe Dispenza, but its first two minutes are
   Heart. The library adds HeartMath Institute (Quick Coherence).
3. No citation in the brief is wrong. Details worth knowing:
   - Balban and others is _Cell Reports Medicine_ 4(1):100895, published January 2023, from
     Stanford. Its participants were mostly Stanford students.
   - "Name it to tame it" is Dan Siegel's phrase, from _The Whole-Brain Child_ (2011, with Tina
     Payne Bryson). The research behind the idea is Matthew Lieberman's (2007), not Siegel's own.
   - Mark Divine popularised box breathing; he did not invent it. Equal counts of in, hold, out and
     hold is an old pranayama (sama vritti).
   - Quick Coherence is a registered trademark of HeartMath. The credit names it; the app must not
     present ECHO's practice as HeartMath's.
   - "Glimmers" is Deb Dana's term, first used in _The Polyvagal Theory in Therapy_ (2018).
4. **Heart's bell** comes after about a minute of heart breathing, at every length, not at the
   halfway mark, for the reasons given under Heart below. This changes the brief's design, not a
   citation.

## Safety for every practice

- **Dizziness.** Slow, full breathing can make some people lightheaded, more so with big breaths in
  or with holds. On a person's first practice the app says "If you feel dizzy or unwell, stop and
  breathe normally." Every practice is safe to stop at any moment, and none asks for big breaths.
- **Breath holds** are only in Sleep (seven seconds, four times) and in the box breathing of One
  thing and Ready (four seconds). Holding the breath briefly raises blood pressure. Someone who is
  pregnant, or has a heart condition, high blood pressure that is not controlled, or a lung
  condition, can breathe slowly through the holds instead, or choose a practice without them. The
  app does not say this yet; the lead may want it on the "before it starts" screen of those three.
- **Panic and the heart.** A panic attack can feel like a heart problem, and a heart problem can
  feel like panic. ECHO must never tell anyone that chest pain or breathlessness is "only panic". New,
  different or severe chest pain, fainting, or breathlessness that does not ease needs medical help.
- **Near a crisis.** Hurt, low mood and urges can sit close to one. The support panel follows
  "Still unsettled" after Ground and Soothe (`afterUnsettled: 'support'`), and You always reaches it.
- **Not therapy.** None of these practices replace care for anxiety, panic, depression, insomnia or
  addiction.

## Settle (Anxious, 3 minutes, free)

**In the app.** Cyclic sighing: in through the nose for 3 seconds, a short second breath in on top
for 1, out through the mouth for 6 (six breaths a minute). The last minute slows to an 8 second
breath out, and at two minutes the voice asks for one silent word for the feeling.

**Sources.**

- Balban MY, Neri E, Kogon MM, Weed L, Nouriani B, Jo B, Holl G, Zeitzer JM, Spiegel D, Huberman AD.
  Brief structured respiration practices enhance mood and reduce physiological arousal. _Cell
  Reports Medicine_. 2023;4(1):100895. doi:10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895
- Siegel DJ, Bryson TP. _The Whole-Brain Child_. Delacorte Press; 2011. ("Name it to tame it.")
- Lieberman MD, Eisenberger NI, Crockett MJ, Tom SM, Pfeifer JH, Way BM. Putting feelings into
  words: affect labeling disrupts amygdala activity in response to affective stimuli. _Psychological
  Science_. 2007;18(5):421-428.

**What they teach.** In Balban's month long remote trial, people spent five minutes a day on one of
three breathing practices or on mindfulness meditation. Cyclic sighing was a slow breath in, a
second shorter breath in to fill the lungs, then a slow, full breath out, the breaths in through the
nose and the breath out through the mouth. It raised daily positive mood more than mindfulness did,
and lowered resting breathing rate more. Siegel teaches that putting words to a big feeling helps
it settle; Lieberman found that naming the feeling in a picture lowered the amygdala's response to
it, in the lab.

**Evidence: Some.** Balban: 108 adults, 30 of them in cyclic sighing, without moderate or severe
psychiatric conditions; exploratory, registered after the fact, remote and self reported. Anxiety
and low mood fell in every group, mindfulness included, with no difference between groups. Naming:
one brain imaging study and later lab work, with modest effects.

**ECHO must not claim** that sighing lowers anxiety more than other methods, that it is "proven" or
"Stanford's", anything about cortisol, the vagus nerve or the amygdala, or that naming a feeling
makes it go. The "why" says "lifted mood more than mindfulness meditation did" and "tends to".

**Safety.** The double breath in fills the lungs; anyone lightheaded can make the breaths smaller or
stop. No holds.

## Ground (Panicky, 3 minutes, free, support panel after)

**In the app.** No hold at any point (a test enforces it). It starts close to a quick breath (in 2,
out 5) and slows over the first minute to in 3, out 7. The breaths in stay small and the breaths out
long, "as if through a straw". At one minute: look around and find three things you can see; at two:
feel the floor; waves like this pass.

**Sources.**

- Levine PA. _In an Unspoken Voice_. North Atlantic Books; 2010. Also _Waking the Tiger_ (1997).
- Dana D. _The Polyvagal Theory in Therapy_. W. W. Norton; 2018. _Anchored_. Sounds True; 2021.
- Meuret AE, Rosenfield D, Seidel A, Bhaskara L, Hofmann SG. Respiratory and cognitive mediators of
  treatment change in panic disorder: evidence for intervention specificity. _Journal of Consulting
  and Clinical Psychology_. 2010;78(5):691-704.

**What they teach.** Levine treats orienting, the body's own turning toward its surroundings with the
head and eyes, as part of how it comes out of alarm, and uses slow, curious orienting to the room in
front of you to settle high arousal. Dana teaches orienting to the room around you and to small
cues of safety in it. Feeling the floor under your feet is a common grounding step in the same
spirit. Meuret's work shows that in panic people tend to overbreathe, which
lowers carbon dioxide and feeds the symptoms; training slower and shallower breathing, not deep
breaths, corrected it, and panic eased as much as with cognitive training.

**How the app uses it.** The straw slows the breath out. Small breaths in avoid the overbreathing
that "take a deep breath" can make worse. Orienting gives attention somewhere outside the alarm. A
panic attack rises to a peak and falls, so "Waves like this pass" is true.

**Evidence.** Some for slow, shallow breathing in panic (Meuret: 41 patients over four weeks, with
a capnometer giving feedback; small). Teaching only for orienting as a self help step. The
polyvagal theory behind Dana's language is contested (see Lift).

**ECHO must not claim** that it stops a panic attack, that it is safe because it is "only anxiety",
that it calms the vagus nerve or "tells the body it is safe", and must never say "you are safe": ECHO
cannot know.

**Safety.** No holds. If slow breathing feels like too little air, any easy pace is fine; nothing in
the voice demands. See "Panic and the heart" above.

## Soothe (Hurting, 4 minutes, free, support panel after)

**In the app.** A hand on the heart, "if you like". Slow breaths with a longer breath out (in 3, out
5, then in 4, out 6), no holds. Three kind sentences a minute apart: this is hard; others feel it
too; then something kind, as you would say to a friend. Then quiet.

**Sources.**

- Neff K. _Self-Compassion_. William Morrow; 2011.
- Neff KD, Germer CK. A pilot study and randomized controlled trial of the Mindful Self-Compassion
  program. _Journal of Clinical Psychology_. 2013;69(1):28-44.
- Ferrari M, Hunt C, Harrysunker A, Abbott MJ, Beath AP, Einstein DA. Self-compassion interventions
  and psychosocial outcomes: a meta-analysis of RCTs. _Mindfulness_. 2019;10:1455-1473.
- Dreisoerner A, Junker NM, Schlotz W, Heimrich J, Bloemeke S, Ditzen B, van Dick R. Self-soothing
  touch and being hugged reduce cortisol responses to stress. _Comprehensive
  Psychoneuroendocrinology_. 2021;8:100091.

**What they teach.** Neff's self-compassion break, from the Mindful Self-Compassion program she
built with Christopher Germer, is a short pause in a hard moment with three parts: noticing that this
is a moment of pain, remembering that pain is part of every human life, and offering yourself the
kindness you would offer a friend. A soothing touch, such as a hand on the chest, can go with it.
ECHO's three sentences are its own words for those three parts, not Neff's phrases.

**Evidence.** Good for self-compassion programs: Ferrari and others pooled 27 trials and found
moderate effects on stress, self-criticism, depression and anxiety. The short break has not been
tested alone as a few minute exercise. Touch: Some, from one lab trial of 159 healthy young adults,
where self-soothing touch lowered cortisol after a stress test, but not self-reported stress or
heart rate. The "why" says only that touch "can help the kindness feel real".

**ECHO must not claim** that it heals grief, trauma or depression, or that touch lowers stress
hormones, and must never tell someone to stop being hard on themselves or that their pain is small.

**Safety.** Germer and Neff call it backdraft: kindness can at first bring old pain up rather than
down. That is why the hand is optional, the sentences are offered rather than required, there is a
minute between them, and "Still unsettled" leads to the support panel.

## Heart (Wanting to feel grateful, 5 minutes, free at 3 and 5)

**In the app.** Breathing at the person's own pace (5.5 breaths a minute to start; Plus chooses 4.5
to 6.5), in for 40 percent of each breath and out for 60, "as if through your heart". At the bell,
remember a moment of gratitude or love and feel it again; one reminder brings it back if it fades.

**Why the bell comes at about 1:10, not half way.** HeartMath's own technique brings the feeling in
after only half a minute or so of heart breathing and then asks you to hold it, and someone who
chose "Wanting to feel grateful" should spend most of the practice feeling it. So the feeling comes
at about 1:10 at every length, a reminder follows a minute later, and a longer practice holds the
feeling longer: about 3:45 of feeling at 5 minutes, and 1:45 at 3. A bell at exactly half way would
also break the free 3 minute Heart: only one step can grow or shrink with the length, so it would
leave either two lines 30 seconds apart (the voice speaks at most once a minute) or 25 seconds of
feeling at the end.

**Sources.**

- HeartMath Institute. The Quick Coherence Technique (heartmath.org).
- Lehrer PM, Gevirtz R. Heart rate variability biofeedback: how and why does it work? _Frontiers in
  Psychology_. 2014;5:756.
- Goessl VC, Curtiss JE, Hofmann SG. The effect of heart rate variability biofeedback training on
  stress and anxiety: a meta-analysis. _Psychological Medicine_. 2017;47(15):2578-2586.
- Dispenza J. _Breaking the Habit of Being Yourself_. Hay House; 2012. _Becoming Supernatural_. Hay
  House; 2017.
- Davis DE, Choe E, Meyers J, and others. Thankful for the little things: a meta-analysis of
  gratitude interventions. _Journal of Counseling Psychology_. 2016;63(1):20-31.

**What they teach.** HeartMath's Quick Coherence, as its page sets it out: put your attention on the
area of the heart; breathe as if the breath moves in and out of that area, a little slower than
usual, for about 30 to 40 seconds; then sincerely try to feel a warm, renewing feeling, such as
appreciation or care for a person, a pet, a place or something you did, and hold it. Dispenza teaches bringing up elevated emotions, such as gratitude, love and
joy, and feeling them as if what you hope for has already happened. Lehrer and Gevirtz describe how
breathing near six breaths a minute makes the heart rate rise and fall with each breath in large,
smooth waves.

**Evidence.** Some to Good for slow breathing near six a minute: Goessl and others pooled 24 studies
of heart rate variability biofeedback (484 people) and found large drops in self-reported stress and
anxiety, though most used a biofeedback device and many were small. ECHO uses no device and measures
nothing. Gratitude exercises: Some, small. Davis and others found better wellbeing than doing nothing
(d = 0.31) but not better than other active exercises. HeartMath's wider claims about heart and brain
"coherence" go beyond independent evidence, and Dispenza's accounts of the brain, genes and healing
are not supported by independent research.

**ECHO must not claim** to measure anything (no heart rate, no variability, no "coherence"; the word
should not appear in the app's copy), anything about heart health or blood pressure, any of
Dispenza's claims about genes, the quantum field or healing, or that gratitude changes what happens.

**Safety.** No holds. A pace slower than comfortable can bring air hunger or lightheadedness; the
brief's rule stands: the pace that feels easy, never forced.

## Cool (Angry, 3 minutes, Plus)

**In the app.** In through the nose for 4, then out with a quiet hum, lips closed, for 6 and then 8.
The voice says you do not have to answer yet, and later that you can answer later and choose your
words then.

**Sources.**

- Maltz M. _Psycho-Cybernetics_. Prentice-Hall; 1960. (The delayed reaction.)
- Bhramari pranayama, the humming bee breath, from yoga.
- Kjærvik SL, Bushman BJ. A meta-analytic review of anger management activities that increase or
  decrease arousal: what fuels or douses rage? _Clinical Psychology Review_. 2024.
- Kuppusamy M, Kamaldeen D, Pitani R, Amaldas J, Shanmugam P. Effects of Bhramari Pranayama on
  health: a systematic review. _Journal of Traditional and Complementary Medicine_.
  2018;8(1):11-16.
- Weitzberg E, Lundberg JON. Humming greatly increases nasal nitric oxide. _American Journal of
  Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine_. 2002;166(2):144-145.

**What they teach.** Maltz describes much of our reacting as a conditioned reflex, like jumping up to
answer a ringing telephone. We can learn to delay the response, to let it ring, which breaks the
automatic reaction and leaves room to choose. Bhramari is a breath in, then a slow breath out with a
steady hum, lips closed, attending to the sound and its vibration. Kjærvik and Bushman pooled 154
studies (about 10,000 people): activities that calm the body, such as slow breathing, relaxation,
mindfulness and taking a timeout, reduced anger and aggression; venting and activities that raise
arousal did not.

**Evidence.** Good that calming the body helps with anger. Teaching only for the delayed reaction
itself. Thin for bhramari: Kuppusamy and others found six small studies, none randomised, all of very
low quality. Humming raises nitric oxide in the nose; nothing links that to mood.

**ECHO must not claim** anything about nitric oxide, the vagus nerve, blood pressure or the heart,
that anger is wrong or must be pushed down, or that it treats anger problems.

**Safety.** No holds. With a blocked nose, breathe in through the mouth. The hum stays quiet and
easy, never strained.

## One thing (Overwhelmed, 4 minutes, Plus)

**In the app.** Box breathing, four counts in, hold, out and hold, for 80 seconds. Then the counting
stops, an easy breath (in 4, out 6) carries four lines, 20 seconds apart, that build a small quiet
room inside you, and the voice leaves you in it.

**Sources.**

- Mark Divine, former US Navy SEAL, who popularised box breathing through his SEALFIT and
  Unbeatable Mind teaching.
- Balban and others, 2023 (above), whose box breathing group used equal counts of 3 to 6 seconds.
- Maltz M. _Psycho-Cybernetics_. 1960. (The quiet room.)

**What they teach.** Divine teaches equal counts of breathing in, holding, breathing out and holding
to steady the mind under pressure. Maltz teaches building, in imagination, a small, simple, restful
room, with soft colours, somewhere comfortable to sit and nothing that calls for attention, and
going there for short rests from the day's demands, often enough that it becomes familiar.

**Evidence.** Some for box breathing: in Balban's trial, anxiety fell about as much with five
minutes a day of box breathing as with mindfulness (21 people in the box breathing group); its gain
in mood was not significantly different from mindfulness. Teaching only for the quiet room.

**ECHO must not claim** that it is "how Navy SEALs stay calm" (marketing, and it implies an
endorsement), or that the room removes stress. "It can wait a few minutes" is the length of a rest,
never advice that real obligations can be ignored.

**Safety.** Four second holds: see "Breath holds" above. If counting feels like too much, breathe
normally; the room still works.

## Sleep (Can't sleep, 10 minutes, Plus)

**In the app.** Four rounds of in 4, hold 7, out 8 through the mouth with a soft whoosh, Weil's own
limit of four breaths. Then no more holds: in 4, out 8 for seven breaths, then in 4, out 6 to the
end. The voice stops after its third line, at about 2:50, the screen fades to black over a minute,
and there is no closing bell to wake anyone.

**Sources.**

- Andrew Weil, the 4-7-8 or "relaxing" breath (drweil.com), adapted by Weil from pranayama.
- Tsai HJ, Kuo TBJ, Lee GS, Yang CCH. Efficacy of paced breathing for insomnia: enhances vagal
  activity and improves sleep quality. _Psychophysiology_. 2015;52(3):388-396.
- Vierra J, Boonla O, Prasertsri P. Effects of sleep deprivation and 4-7-8 breathing control on heart
  rate variability, blood pressure, blood glucose, and endothelial function in healthy young adults.
  _Physiological Reports_. 2022:e15389.

**What they teach.** Weil: the tip of the tongue on the ridge behind the upper front teeth, a full
breath out through the mouth with a whoosh, in quietly through the nose for 4, hold for 7, out
through the mouth for 8; four breaths; the ratio matters more than the speed, and no more than four
breaths at first. ECHO leaves out the tongue to keep the voice spare.

**Evidence.** Thin for 4-7-8 itself: Vierra and others measured one session in 43 young adults and
found changes in heart rate variability and blood pressure, with no sleep outcome. Some for slow
breathing before bed: in Tsai and others, 14 people with insomnia who breathed slowly (six a minute)
for 20 minutes before bed fell asleep sooner, woke less and slept more of the night. Small.

**ECHO must not claim** "asleep in 60 seconds" (a popular claim with no evidence) or that it treats
insomnia. Insomnia that lasts deserves a clinician.

**Safety.** The seven second hold: see "Breath holds" above. Weil notes lightheadedness at first is
common; breathing normally through the holds is fine.

## Lift (Low or flat, 3 minutes, Plus)

**In the app.** Sit up a little, "if you can". In 4, out 4, with each breath in a little fuller,
then a little quicker, in 3, out 3. Then, with an easy breath (in 4, out 5), find one small thing
that is still okay. No holds.

**Sources.**

- Porges SW. _The Polyvagal Theory_. W. W. Norton; 2011.
- Dana D. _The Polyvagal Theory in Therapy_. 2018. _Anchored_. 2021.
- Wilkes C, Kydd R, Sagar M, Broadbent E. Upright posture improves affect and fatigue in people with
  depressive symptoms. _Journal of Behavior Therapy and Experimental Psychiatry_. 2017;54:143-149.
- Nair S, Sagar M, Sollers J, Consedine N, Broadbent E. Do slumped and upright postures affect stress
  responses? A randomized trial. _Health Psychology_. 2015;34(6):632-641.
- Grossman P. Fundamental challenges and likely refutations of the five basic premises of the
  polyvagal theory. _Biological Psychology_. 2023;180:108589.

**What they teach.** Porges describes the body moving, below awareness, between safety and
connection, mobilisation, and shutdown (flat, heavy, withdrawn). Dana teaches leaving shutdown gently
and in small steps, first toward a little energy and movement and then toward connection, rather
than forcing calm or cheer, and noticing glimmers, small moments of ease or okayness, so that they
register.

**How the app uses it.** Sitting up and fuller, slightly quicker breaths are a small step toward
energy; the glimmer is a step toward something okay.

**Evidence.** Some for posture: in Wilkes and others, people with mild to moderate depressive
symptoms who sat upright for a short lab task had more energy and less fatigue than those who sat as
usual; in Nair and others (74 adults), people sitting upright under stress reported better mood,
higher self-esteem and less fear than people slumped. Teaching only for glimmers. The polyvagal
theory is contested: Grossman argues each of its five premises is untenable or highly implausible.
ECHO uses its words as a vocabulary for states, never as a claim about the nervous system.

**ECHO must not claim** to treat or help depression, anything about the vagus nerve or "ventral" and
"dorsal" states, or that looking on the bright side fixes low mood.

**Safety.** Quicker, fuller breaths can bring lightheadedness if they get big; breathe normally if
so. Low mood that lasts two weeks or more, or any thought of self harm, needs a person: the support
panel in You. The brief offers the panel after Ground and Soothe only; the lead may want it after
Lift too.

## Ready (Before something hard, 4 minutes, Plus)

**In the app.** Box breathing, four counts each, for 1:52 (seven boxes). Then, over an easy breath,
the voice guides you to remember a time you did something hard and it went well, a line every 16 or
17 seconds, and leaves you to feel it again.

**Sources.**

- Maltz M. _Psycho-Cybernetics_. 1960. (The winning feeling.)
- Mark Divine (box breathing), as in One thing.
- Speer ME, Delgado MR. Reminiscing about positive memories buffers acute stress responses. _Nature
  Human Behaviour_. 2017;1:0093.
- Bandura A. Self-efficacy: toward a unifying theory of behavioral change. _Psychological Review_.
  1977;84(2):191-215.

**What they teach.** Maltz: before something new or hard, recall a past success in vivid detail
until you feel that success again; the feeling, more than the facts, carries into what you do next.
Bandura: having done something hard before is one of the main sources of believing you can do it
again.

**Evidence.** Some: Speer and Delgado (134 people) found that recalling happy memories after a
stressor dampened the cortisol rise and lowered negative mood, where neutral memories did not.
Teaching only for the winning feeling as Maltz describes it; self-efficacy is a well established
theory, not a test of this exercise.

**ECHO must not claim** that it improves performance or makes an outcome likely, or anything about
cortisol beyond "the body's response to stress".

**Safety.** Box holds: see "Breath holds" above. "It can be small" keeps the door open for someone
who cannot think of a big success.

## Ride the wave (A craving or an urge, 3 minutes, Plus)

**In the app.** Slow, easy breaths (in 4, out 6). Notice the urge and where it sits in the body;
watch it rise like a wave without acting; urges rise, peak and fall.

**Sources.**

- Marlatt GA, Gordon JR, editors. _Relapse Prevention: Maintenance Strategies in the Treatment of
  Addictive Behaviors_. Guilford Press; 1985.
- Bowen S, Marlatt A. Surfing the urge: brief mindfulness-based intervention for college student
  smokers. _Psychology of Addictive Behaviors_. 2009;23(4):666-671.

**What they teach.** Urges are like waves: they rise, crest and subside. Rather than fighting an
urge or giving in, you notice it in the body with curiosity, use the breath to stay steady, and ride
it until it passes.

**Evidence.** Some: in Bowen and Marlatt, 123 students who smoked and were given brief urge surfing
instructions smoked fewer cigarettes over the next week than those given none, though their urges
were no weaker.

**ECHO must not claim** that it treats addiction or makes urges go, or that three minutes is enough
for an urge to pass. The popular figure that urges pass in 20 to 30 minutes has no source we could
find.

**Safety.** If the urge is to harm yourself, or to use something in a way that is dangerous, it needs
a person: the support panel. Not a replacement for treatment.

## Arrive (Become's morning practice, 4 minutes, free)

**In the app.** Two minutes of Heart at the person's own pace, then the theater of the mind over an
easy breath: picture one ordinary moment of today; see yourself in it as the person you are becoming,
how you stand and move; hear what you say; notice how it feels, as if it is already true. Four lines,
20 seconds apart, then quiet. Two minutes is a whole number of breaths at every pace a person may
choose (9 to 13), so the theater always starts on time.

**Sources.**

- Maltz M. _Psycho-Cybernetics_. 1960. (The theater of the mind.)
- Dispenza J. _Breaking the Habit of Being Yourself_. 2012. (Mental rehearsal.)
- HeartMath Institute, Quick Coherence, as in Heart.
- Driskell JE, Copper C, Moran A. Does mental practice enhance performance? _Journal of Applied
  Psychology_. 1994;79(4):481-492.
- Toth AJ, McNeill E, Hayes K, Moran AP, Campbell M. Does mental practice still enhance performance?
  A 24 year follow-up and meta-analytic replication and extension. _Psychology of Sport and
  Exercise_. 2020.

**What they teach.** Maltz: sit quietly and picture yourself, in vivid detail, acting and feeling the
way you want to, as if it is happening now, in a theater of the mind; practised daily, the new
picture becomes familiar. Dispenza: each morning, rehearse in the mind how you will think, act and
feel as the person you are becoming, until it feels natural, before the day begins.

**Evidence.** Good that mental practice improves performance of the practised task: Driskell and
others' meta-analysis, and Toth and others' 24 year follow-up, which found a small but reliable
effect. Teaching only for rehearsing a way of being, as Maltz and Dispenza mean it.

**ECHO must not claim** that it changes or rewires the brain or makes the future happen, and must
never describe who the person "really" is.

**Safety.** As Heart.

## The voice

- **Model.** `gpt-4o-mini-tts-2025-12-15`, the newest text to speech snapshot that `GET /v1/models`
  listed on 2026-11-08, and the only family that takes style instructions (`tts-1` and `tts-1-hd` do
  not). `gpt-live-1` is not served on `/v1/audio/speech`. The `speed` setting does not work with this
  model, so pace comes from the instructions. The snapshot is pinned so the same words always come
  back in the same voice.
- **Voice.** `shimmer`. Eight voices (shimmer, cedar, marin, sage, ash, ballad, onyx, coral) read
  the same three lines with a first set of instructions, and the six strongest read them again with
  the final ones. Their pitch, pitch movement, pace and brightness were measured from the raw takes.
  Shimmer had the steadiest pitch of the voices that are not a deep bass (about 2.2 semitones of
  spread, where marin moved 3.1), the warmest, softest tone after onyx, and an unhurried pace (about
  2.2 words a second, where cedar and marin ran near 2.5 even when asked to slow down). Sage and ash
  were slower but brighter and less steady; onyx was steadier still, but a deep narrator's voice.
  OpenAI recommends marin and cedar for the most natural sound, which these measures cannot judge,
  so they are the two alternatives for the owner to hear.
- **No one listened.** The voice was chosen without being heard. An audio model asked to describe the
  takes rated every one the same, and missed loud clicks and a cut off line planted to test it, so it
  had no part in the choice. The owner's ear decides.
- **Every take is checked.** Each line is written down again by `gpt-transcribe`, and a take whose
  words differ from the line is made again. In the audition, one take said "Breathe it out" for "Let
  it out" and was redone. Takes of the same words also vary in length by a second or more, so a take
  of a breathing line that would run past the out breath it starts on, at any pace, is made again
  too: one take of settle.open ran 6.5 seconds against its 6 second breath out and was redone.
- **Processing.** Silence trimmed to 150 ms at each end, short fades, integrated loudness brought to
  -20 LUFS (ITU-R BS.1770-4), a look ahead limiter keeping peaks at -3 dBFS or lower, mono AAC at 64
  kbps in .m4a by `afconvert`, and the peak checked again after encoding. ffmpeg's EBU R128 meter
  agrees: every line is -20.0 to -20.2 LUFS, with true peaks from -6.5 to -3.3 dBTP.
- **Disclosure.** OpenAI's usage policies ask that people are told plainly that a text to speech
  voice is AI generated and not a human voice. `SYNTHETIC_VOICE_NOTE` in the library says so for You.
  The raw audio carries no metadata to keep, only its format and samples.
- **Changing it.** Set `VOICE` or `INSTRUCTIONS` in `scripts/generate-breath-voice.mjs` and run it;
  every line is made again, because the voice settings are part of each line's hash. The tests then
  check that every breathing line still ends before the next breath in, which a slower voice may not.
